Abstract

This report describes the design and simulation of an industry-standard autonomous mobile robot (AMR), the AgileX Scout Mini four-wheel skid-steer platform, as a warehouse robot in MATLAB R2025b with Simulink and Simscape Multibody. The robot was modelled in SolidWorks (Assem2 with the parts chassis, chassis_top, W and up), exported to the Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) with STL meshes, and imported into Simulink with the Simscape Multibody function smimport and into the Robotics System Toolbox with importrobot. The imported model was turned into a working dynamic plant: the chassis is a free 6-DOF body, the four wheels are torque-driven revolute joints, and a custom tyre-ground contact model (spring-damper normal force with smoothed Coulomb friction) supplies the forces, including low-friction oil and water patches on the floor. A 3D warehouse (nine storage racks with goods, walls, pickup and delivery stations, floor boxes) is part of the multibody scene, together with four moving obstacles (two workers, a second AMR and a pallet truck). The robot is autonomous: a simulated 360° LiDAR feeds an occupancy grid, an A* planner finds and re-plans routes, a pure-pursuit controller follows them, and a mission state machine runs the sequence start, pickup, load, delivery, unload. A wheel-slip management layer (slip-ratio estimation, torque limiting, traction-control window and friction estimation) keeps the wheels from spinning or locking. In the reference run the robot completed the mission in 49.6 s over 32.0 m with 3 route corrections, 0 collisions and a minimum clearance of 0.22 m. The results are simulation only: the pose comes from the simulator (no localisation), LiDAR detection and collision checks are analytic computations on the warehouse geometry, the moving obstacles follow scripted trajectories, and no test was done on the physical robot. These limits are repeated in the relevant sections.

1. Introduction

An autonomous mobile robot (AMR) senses its surroundings, builds a representation of them, decides where to go and moves there without a fixed track or a human driver. In a warehouse an AMR carries goods between stations, and it must avoid racks, boxes, forklifts and people. Unlike an automated guided vehicle (AGV), which follows wires, tape or magnets, an AMR plans its own route and re-plans when the route is blocked. Testing such a robot on a real warehouse floor is slow and risky, so the software is first developed in simulation.

The robot used in this project is the AgileX Scout Mini, a compact four-wheel skid-steer chassis. The chassis and wheels were modelled by the author in SolidWorks (assembly Assem2) and converted to URDF. The project went through the following stages, which are also the structure of this report:

  • SolidWorks CAD model of the robot, export to URDF and STL meshes (sections 4 and 5);
  • import into MATLAB with importrobot and into Simulink with smimport (Simscape Multibody) (section 6);
  • turning the import into a physical plant: free chassis, torque-driven wheels, tyre-ground friction (section 7);
  • a 3D warehouse with racks, goods, stations, spills, static boxes and moving workers, robot and truck (section 8);
  • autonomous navigation: LiDAR, occupancy grid, A* planning, re-planning, path following, mission logic (sections 9 and 11);
  • wheel-slip detection and management, with a comparison of slip management on and off (section 10);
  • an earlier MATLAB-script simulation of the same robot with ISO 3691-4 style safety fields (section 14);
  • documentation of the code and the commands so that the work can be repeated (section 15).

The scope is simulation only. The wheel-ground forces, the wheel dynamics and the chassis motion are simulated by Simscape Multibody. The LiDAR, the obstacle geometry and the collision check are software models. Localisation error, battery and motor electrical models, the real Scout Mini firmware and hardware tests are not covered.

5. Conversion to URDF

The Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) is an XML file that describes a robot as a tree of links (rigid parts with mass, inertia, visual and collision shapes) and joints (how a child link is attached to its parent: type, origin and axis). An STL file holds only the triangle mesh of a shape; the URDF says where each mesh sits and how it moves. The meshes are in metres.

The assembly was exported with the SolidWorks-to-URDF exporter into the ROS 2 package assem2_robot of the author's repository AgileX_ScoutMini_JAZZY (urdf/Assem2.SLDASM.urdf and meshes/base_link.STL, w1.STL…w4.STL). That URDF was later edited by hand for the ROS 2 / Gazebo project: it carries analytic collision shapes (a box for the chassis, cylinders for the wheels), an adjusted low centre of mass, fixed mounts for the LiDAR, IMU and camera, and Gazebo plug-ins and sensor definitions. For the MATLAB work the file was copied to scout_mini.urdf, and the only change was to replace the ROS path package://assem2_robot/meshes/ by the relative path meshes/, because MATLAB does not know the package:// convention.

6. Importing in MATLAB and Simulink

The robot was imported in two ways. First, importrobot (Robotics System Toolbox) turns the URDF into a rigidBodyTree, which is used for visualisation and to read the wheel transforms. Second, smimport (Simscape Multibody) turns the same URDF into a Simulink model made of Simscape Multibody blocks: Solid blocks with the STL geometry, Rigid Transforms for the joint frames, Revolute Joints for the wheels and a Weld Joint for the base footprint.

An important property of the imported model: the joint frames created by smimport have their Z axis along the wheel axle (not the X axis written in the URDF). The tyre model and the slip-ratio sign convention were built on this fact; using the wrong axis had put the wheel hubs up to 10 cm off and tilted the chassis at the start of the simulation.

7. Building the Simscape Multibody Model

The raw import is a rigid, welded robot. build_scout_amr_model.m turns it into a dynamic plant by the following steps, which are executed in the order given and saved as scout_amr_sim.slx.

  • The Weld Joint between base_footprint and base_link is replaced by a 6-DOF Joint, so the chassis can translate and rotate freely in the world.
  • The four wheel revolute joints (j1…j4) get TorqueActuationMode = InputTorque (the controller applies a torque to every wheel) and SenseVelocity = on.
  • Transform Sensor blocks measure the world-frame pose and velocity of the chassis and of each wheel (position, velocity, rotation, angular velocity), and Simulink-PS / PS-Simulink converters connect them to the signal domain.
  • Four External Force and Torque blocks, one per wheel, apply the tyre forces computed by the tyre-ground model.
  • The controller and the tyre model are MATLAB Function blocks that call scout_amr_controller.m and scout_amr_tires.m. They run at the control period of 5 ms through zero-order-hold blocks.
  • The 3D warehouse and the moving obstacles are added as Brick and Cylinder Solid blocks (section 8).
  • Logging blocks store the controller, tyre, LiDAR, path and mover signals (To Workspace).
  • Solver: ode23t (variable step, maximum step 0.01 s), stop time 120 s; the simulation stops itself when the mission is done.

7.1 Tyre-ground contact model

The Scout Mini wheels roll on the floor, so the multibody model needs forces between the wheels and the ground. Simscape Multibody has no built-in friction contact for this case, therefore a contact model was written (scout_amr_tires.m) and is evaluated once for every wheel:

  • Contact point: below the wheel hub on the axis perpendicular to the axle, at the rolling radius r = 0.085 m. The hub position follows from the wheel frame position plus 0.062 m along the axle.
  • Normal force: Fz = kc·δ + cc·δ̇ (never negative) with kc = 12 000 N/m, cc = 700 N·s/m per wheel; δ is the penetration of the tyre into the floor.
  • Friction: the contact-point velocity gives a sliding velocity; the tangential force is μ·Fz scaled by a smoothed sign function with smoothing velocity v0 = 0.03 m/s (smoothed Coulomb friction).
  • μ comes from a floor map: μ = 0.9 on dry concrete, 0.15 on the oily loading dock at the start, and 0.30 on two water spills in the aisles.
  • Application: the force is resolved in the wheel frame and applied at the wheel-frame origin through the External Force and Torque block, together with the moment of the force about the offset contact point.
  • Diagnostics for each wheel: Fz, longitudinal force, lateral force, slip ratio and μ (11 outputs per wheel in the log).

With 30 kg of robot mass the static load per wheel is about 74 N, which compresses the contact by about 6 mm; this agrees with the normal-load trace in Fig. 15 (60–80 N when driving straight).

The model is organised so that its top level stays small. It holds only the World Frame, the Mechanism Configuration, the Solver Configuration and four colour-coded subsystems (Fig. 6); all signals between the subsystems travel through Goto / From tags, so the top level contains no signal wires, only the physical frame connections of the multibody tree.

9.3 Limits of these results

  • Simulation only; no run on the physical robot. The pose is the true pose of the simulator (no localisation or odometry error).
  • One layout and one mission. The simulation is deterministic, so repeating it gives the same numbers; the result shows that the chain works, not that it works on every layout.
  • The workers are partly cooperative: they pause when the AMR is close. They are still obstacles that the robot must detect and avoid, but they are gentler than real traffic.
  • The LiDAR and the collision check are analytic computations on the warehouse geometry. The robot does not collide physically with the racks or the movers: there is no Simscape contact between the robot and the obstacles, and the movers cannot be pushed.
  • The minimum clearance of 0.22 m is measured from the robot's collision circle (r = 0.36 m), which is a simplification of the real chassis outline.

10. Wheel Slip and Traction Management

The floor of the warehouse is not uniform, so the wheels can lose grip. The simulation includes three friction zones (dry concrete, an oily loading dock at the start and water spills in two aisles), and a skid-steer robot also scrubs its wheels sideways in every turn. The controller therefore monitors the slip of every wheel and manages it.

10.1 How slip is detected and managed

  • Slip ratio of each wheel: κ = (r·ω − v_k) / max(|r·ω|, |v_k|, 0.8 m/s). Here ω is the wheel speed, r the radius and v_k the ground speed at the wheel's lateral position (chassis speed minus yaw rate times the wheel offset). The 0.8 m/s floor avoids division by zero at standstill.
  • Detection: a wheel slips when |κ| > 0.12; a slip timer records how long this lasts.
  • Traction-control window: the wheel-speed target is limited to the speed that matches the ground ± a tolerance (anti-spin on acceleration, anti-lock on braking). The allowed slip is regulated to about 0.08.
  • Torque limiting: above the threshold the available torque is reduced linearly, down to 10 % of the 17.5 N·m limit at κ = 0.40.
  • Friction estimation: while slip is detected the friction estimate μ̂ decreases by 0.5 per second (not below 0.10); when there is no slip it recovers at 0.15 per second up to 0.9.
  • Friction-aware acceleration: the acceleration demand is limited to 0.85·μ̂·g, so the robot accelerates gently on a slippery floor.
  • Wheel-speed control: a PI controller (Kp = 0.3, Ki = 8) with an acceleration feed-forward term (30 kg · a · r / 4) drives each wheel to its target; the integrator is clamped to the current torque limit.

The friction estimate falls as low as 0.10 in the low-friction zones and recovers on dry floor, following the true friction with a lag of several seconds (Fig. 15, bottom left). The slip ratio stays mostly within ±0.2 with short spikes during sharp turns, reversing and in the spills. The torque and normal-load traces are noisy because the contact model is stiff and the controller works at 5 ms; this chatter was not tuned away.

11.1 A* global planner

A* expands the cell with the lowest f(n) = g(n) + h(n), where g is the cost so far and h the straight-line distance to the goal, which never overestimates the remaining cost. A move costs 1 for a straight step and √2 for a diagonal step. A binary heap holds the open set. After the search, line-of-sight shortcuts remove unnecessary waypoints, so the path becomes a few straight segments (the grey paths in Fig. 11).

11.2 Dynamic obstacles

Moving obstacles are handled in a reactive way. Every LiDAR scan marks the hit cells in the dynamic layer for 2 s, and the planner re-plans every 0.5 s or at once when the current path crosses an occupied cell. The motion of a moving obstacle is not predicted. If the path is blocked and an obstacle is closing in, the robot stops or backs away before it re-plans, because the stop-before-contact limit never allows a speed from which the robot could not stop in front of the nearest return.

12. System Architecture

Fig. 17 shows how the pieces connect. The Simscape Multibody plant contains the robot, the warehouse and the movers. The sensors deliver pose, speed and wheel speeds; the world model and the LiDAR deliver the obstacles; the autonomy block plans and decides; the drive control and the slip management turn the commands into wheel torques; and the tyre-ground model turns the wheel states into forces on the wheels. The pose is the true simulator pose, so the loop has no localisation block.

13. What Happens When the Code Runs

run_scout_warehouse_simulation.m loads the model, sets tc_on, simulates, extracts the logs, draws the figures and prints the summary. Inside the simulation, the controller block runs every 5 ms as shown in Fig. 20.

  • Setup: the model is loaded, the stop time is set to 120 s, and tc_on is set to 1 (or 0 for the comparison). The Mechanics Explorer shows the 3D scene when requested.
  • Every 5 ms: pose, velocity and wheel speeds are sampled; the world is evaluated at the current time (the truck is released when the robot passes x = 12 m); the LiDAR is scanned at 10 Hz; the occupancy grid is updated.
  • Every 0.5 s (or at once when the path is blocked): A* is run again from the current pose to the current goal.
  • Control: pure pursuit gives the speed and yaw-rate references; they are shaped by the repulsion, the stop-before-contact limit and the friction-aware acceleration limit, converted into wheel targets, and the wheel-speed PI with slip management gives the four wheel torques.
  • Physics: the tyre-ground model computes the forces, Simscape Multibody integrates the multibody equations with ode23t and moves the robot; the movers follow their scripted motion.
  • End: when the mission state is done, the simulation stops itself; the logs are converted into the figures of this report.

15. How to Set It Up

Requirements: MATLAB R2025b with Simulink, Simscape and Simscape Multibody (the model uses the sm_lib and nesl_utility libraries); the Robotics System Toolbox is used for importrobot and the report figures. Nothing else needs to be installed.

  • Copy the folder simulink_amr (it contains scout_mini.urdf and the meshes folder) to the computer.
  • Open MATLAB, change to the folder and run run_scout_warehouse_simulation. The model scout_amr_sim.slx is built automatically if it is missing.
  • To look at the model, run open_system('scout_amr_sim') and press Run; the Mechanics Explorer shows the warehouse.
  • To switch slip management off, open the subsystem Mission_and_Slip_Controller and set the constant tc_on to 0, or use run_scout_warehouse_simulation(false, true).

16. Limitations and Future Enhancements

16.1 Limitations

  • The pose comes from the simulator: there is no localisation, SLAM or sensor fusion, and the IMU and camera are only mounted, not simulated, in the Simulink model.
  • The LiDAR is analytic and noise-free; collision detection is analytic. There is no Simscape contact between the robot and obstacles.
  • The moving obstacles follow scripted paths; they are partly aware of the robot.
  • The tyre model is a simple point-contact model per wheel; it has no tyre deformation, rolling resistance model or suspension.
  • Motors are ideal torque sources limited to 17.5 N·m; battery, motor electrical dynamics and communication delays are not modelled.
  • Torque and normal-load signals are noisy, and the slip comparison is one run per case.
  • No validation against the real Scout Mini was done.

16.2 Future enhancements

  • Add noisy LiDAR, IMU and wheel odometry as Simulink signals and replace the true pose by an estimator (EKF or AMCL / SLAM).
  • Add contact forces between the robot and the obstacles (Simscape contact or Spatial Contact Force) so that collisions are physical.
  • Model the motors and the battery with Simscape Electrical, and the drive line with Simscape Driveline.
  • Predict the motion of workers and other vehicles instead of reacting to each scan; add fleet traffic rules at the cross-aisles.
  • Test more layouts, random obstacle placements and several runs per slip configuration; tune the controller to reduce torque chatter.
  • Validate the model against measurements of the real Scout Mini (mass, wheel radius, speed limits, slip on real floors).

17. Conclusion

The AgileX Scout Mini was modelled in SolidWorks, exported to URDF and STL, imported into MATLAB with importrobot and into Simulink with smimport, and turned into a dynamic Simscape Multibody plant with a free chassis, torque-driven wheels and a custom tyre-ground friction model. A 3D warehouse with racks, goods, stations, spills, static boxes and four moving obstacles was built into the same multibody scene. The robot navigates autonomously with a LiDAR, an occupancy grid, A* planning and re-planning, pure pursuit and a mission state machine, while a slip-management layer limits wheel slip. In the reference run it delivered the load in 49.6 s with 3 route corrections and 0 collisions, and it handled a floor box, a crossing worker, a blocked aisle and a spill-covered floor. With slip management the peak slip ratio was 0.48 instead of 0.64, at the cost of about 4.1 s of mission time.

These results come from simulation with the true pose, analytic sensing and scripted moving obstacles on a single invented layout, so they show that the software chain works, not that the physical robot would behave the same. The next steps are localisation with noisy sensors, physical contact with obstacles, a motor and battery model, and hardware tests.
